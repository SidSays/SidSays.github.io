Family

Five generations

This family began in a village called Pondil in Bihar and, five generations later, lives in Texas and Bengaluru. Along the way it changed its name twice. This page is what I know, written down before it is lost, and it is unfinished on purpose.

Where the name went

Nath, then Kumar, then Sinha

Two generations of this family were Nath. Then, in the 1940s, the name stopped, and it stopped for two reasons at once.

The first was a curse. Tarkeshwar Nath and Saraswati Devi had two daughters and a series of miscarriages, and they wanted a son. In the Bihar of that time an explanation was ready to hand: the family was cursed, and its boys would not survive marriage. A family priest sent them to the Shiva temples at Deoghar and elsewhere, and gave one further instruction. Change the family name, so that the child would not be born a Nath.

The second reason had nothing to do with temples. We are Kayasthas, then an upper caste, known in Bihar as Lala. Through those decades Kayasthas were killed and looted as caste violence spread through the state, while its politics pushed to reclassify them downward into the Other Backward Classes. Families answered by hiding what a surname announced. The habit outlasted the danger: even now, in 2026, people from Bihar and Jharkhand often carry two first names, or a surname that settles nothing.

Kumar is what those pressures produced. It is a name so common across India that it tells a stranger nothing at all. My father became Sanjay Kumar. I was Siddharth Utsav. My brother was Saharsh Sanyam.

My brother and I took Sinha, our mother's name, and we chose it twice over. We did not want to undo what our ancestors did, since they changed the name to protect the line and Kumar was only ever meant to be a screen. But we wanted to be known as Kayasthas rather than hidden, and Sinha says so plainly. The second reason is simpler. Our mother lost her husband and raised two boys through everything that followed. We carry her name because she earned it.

The people

Rameshwar Nath

Born around the 1890s. Pondil, Bihar.

The furthest back we can see. He lived in Pondil, a village, and the family later moved to Gaya because Gaya was a town. Nothing else has reached me: no occupation, no stories, not a single description.

His wife

Name unknown.

We do not know her name. Somewhere in Gaya a pandit's ledgers may still hold it, written down by a family priest generations ago, and I intend to go and look one day.

Tarkeshwar Nath

Born in the 1920s in Pondil. Died 25 February 1992.

He grew up in Gaya, took a government job as a clerk, and moved to Patna for it. In Gardanibagh he built the house the family settled into, and his four children grew up there.

He was hardworking and straightforward, and he owned a Lambretta scooter that my father kept long after him. I was ridden around Patna on it. Every morning he carried me on his shoulders to Sanjay Gandhi Jaivik Udyan, the zoo, and bought me ramdana laddus on the way. He called me Utsav.

He was not happy at home. His wife and daughters quarrelled constantly and the house kept a bad air about it, which he felt and could not fix. A paralytic attack took him in the end, after two or three years of fighting it. I wish I had been given more time with him.

Saraswati Devi

Born in the 1930s in Sitamarhi, Bihar. Died 15 October 2003.

She came from Sitamarhi and lived in Pondil, Gaya and then Patna. She kept the house, and she worshipped Shiva with a devotion that shaped the family: it was Shiva temples the priest sent them to when they wanted a son.

She loved my father completely. What she wanted for him was that he would be successful and see foreign countries. She did not live to see how far that wish carried: her grandson emigrated to the United States, and her great-grandchildren were born American.

She died of a sudden heart attack, in my arms, at Piska More on Ratu Road in Ranchi. I performed her cremation rites. I loved her, and I wish she and my mother had found some peace between them.

What we keep

Festivals

Holi, Diwali, Navratra, Pitrupaksha and Chhath, and above all Chitragupta Puja, which is the Kayastha festival and the one that ties the family to the caste it once had to hide.

Gaps

What we do not know

The name of Rameshwar Nath's wife. What he did for a living. When exactly he was born, and when he died. Where the ancestral house in Gaya stood, which still exists but is no longer known to me. My father's date of birth, which we hold as 16 September 1954 but have never confirmed.
