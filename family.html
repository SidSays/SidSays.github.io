Family

Five generations

This family began in a village called Pondil in Bihar and, five generations later, lives in Texas and Bengaluru. Along the way it changed its name twice. This page is what I know, written down before it is lost, and it is unfinished on purpose.

Where the name went

Nath, then Kumar, then Sinha

A surname is meant to be the fixed point. It is the part of a name you do not choose, the inheritance that arrives before you do and stays after you have gone. Ours moved twice in three generations. Both times it moved because somebody was afraid.

The first fear was a curse.

In the 1940s, in Bihar, Tarkeshwar Nath and Saraswati Devi wanted a son. Two daughters had come already. Around them came the miscarriages, one and then another, the kind of loss that in that place and that decade was never allowed to stay private. A household counts what it has lost out loud.

The explanation arrived before the grief had finished. The family was cursed. Its boys would not outlive their marriages. Nobody could say who had laid it or when, which is the nature of these things: a curse does not need an origin, only a pattern, and the pattern was there in the empty cradles for anyone who wished to read it.

So they consulted a priest, in the way a family now consults a doctor, and his prescription came in two parts. The first was a journey: the Shiva temples at Deoghar and others scattered across what was then Bihar and is now Jharkhand, stone floors worn smooth by people arriving with exactly this kind of request. The second part was stranger, and more radical. Change the family name. Let the child be born outside the reach of whatever was hunting them. Let him not be a Nath at all.

The second fear had no temple to address it.

We are Kayasthas. In Bihar they call us Lala. It was an upper caste, which is to say it came with a certainty about one's place in a room, and in those same decades the certainty was coming apart. Kayasthas were killed and looted as caste violence worked its way through the state. In the politics of it there was a push to move us downward, to reclassify Kayasthas into the Other Backward Classes, and the argument was conducted as such arguments are, in pamphlets and speeches and then in the street.

Understand what a surname is in India. It is not a label. It is an address. Say it and a stranger knows in a syllable what you are, who your people are, where you may sit, whom you may marry, and what may be done to you without consequence.

So people stopped saying it. Families put their names away the way you put away silver before a war. The habit outlived the danger that made it, which is the way of habits. Even now, in 2026, people from Bihar and Jharkhand carry two first names and no surname worth the term, and nobody finds it remarkable.

Kumar is what that silence sounds like. It is the most ordinary name in India, a name so widely shared that it forecloses every question a stranger might ask. My father went into the world as Sanjay Kumar. I was given Siddharth Utsav. My brother was given Priyaharsh Sanyam. Three men of one household, and not one surname between us that admitted to anything.

Then my brother and I took one, and we took it from our mother.

We are Sinha now. Not to undo what our great-grandparents did, because what they did was protect a line they were frightened of losing, and you do not insult that from the safety of another century. But we are not being hunted. The reason for the hiding has gone, and what remained was a name that said nothing about us. Sinha is a Kayastha name, and it says so plainly. We would rather be known than concealed.

And the other reason has nothing to do with caste or with curses. Our mother lost her husband and raised two boys through everything that came after, alone, without any of it being fair. We carry her name because she is the one who earned it.

There is a last thing, which I only noticed once it was written down. All through the decades when this family could not safely say what it was, it went on worshipping Chitragupta, who belongs to the Kayasthas and to nobody else. The name was hidden in the paperwork. It was never hidden in the house.

The people

Rameshwar Nath

Born in the 1890s. Pondil, Bihar.

He is where the light stops. Beyond him there is nothing: no trade, no story, no word about his face or his temper. He lived in a village called Pondil in the last decade of the nineteenth century, and at some point the family carried his name to Gaya, because Gaya was a town and a town was where a life could grow. That is the whole of him. A name, a village, and a direction of travel.

His wife

Name unknown.

Every person on this page descends from her, and not one of us can say what she was called. Hers is the quietest kind of absence, the kind that leaves nothing to point at. But the ledgers may still hold her. In Gaya the Pandas keep family registers going back generations, column after column of names in a hand that outlived everyone written in it. One day I will sit with one of those books and turn the pages until she appears.

Tarkeshwar Nath

Born in the 1920s in Pondil. Died 25 February 1992.

He grew up in Gaya, took a clerk's job with the government, and moved to Patna because that is where the work was. In Gardanibagh he built a house, and four children grew up inside it.

What I remember is the shoulders. Every morning he lifted me onto them and walked to Sanjay Gandhi Jaivik Udyan, the zoo, buying ramdana laddus along the way, calling me Utsav, which was the name I was born with. There was a Lambretta scooter too, which my father kept long after him, and which carried the two of us through Patna.

The house itself was not peaceful. His wife and daughters quarrelled without end and the air in the rooms stayed sour, and he lived inside it year after year without being able to change it. At the end a paralytic attack took his body by degrees, and he fought it for two or three years before it finished. I have never stopped wishing for more time with him.

Saraswati Devi

Born in the 1930s in Sitamarhi, Bihar. Died 15 October 2003.

She came from Sitamarhi and lived across Pondil, Gaya and Patna, keeping house while the family moved toward the city. Her devotion to Shiva ran underneath everything. When a son was wanted it was Shiva temples the priest sent them to, and it was her faith that carried them there.

She loved my father past all proportion. What she wanted for him was success, and foreign countries, a wish that must have sounded enormous spoken aloud in a Patna household. She did not live long enough to see the size of it: her grandson left for the United States, and her great-grandchildren were born citizens of it.

She died in my arms, of a heart attack that gave no warning, at Piska More on Ratu Road in Ranchi. I performed her cremation rites. I loved her, and I wish she and my mother had made their peace while there was still time to make it.

Beena, Sushma and Sangeeta

Daughters of Tarkeshwar Nath and Saraswati Devi.

My father's three sisters. Beena Kumari married Jugal Kishore Sinha. Sushma Kumari married Tarkeshvar Srivastava. Sangeeta Kumari married Deepak Kumar, and at home she was Pumpi, in the way almost everyone in this family has carried a second and softer name alongside the one on the paperwork.

What we keep

The year still turns on them

Holi, Diwali, Navratra, Pitrupaksha, Chhath. The festivals came down the generations the way furniture does, handled by everyone, belonging to no one in particular.

Above all of them is Chitragupta Puja, which belongs to the Kayasthas. There is something worth sitting with in that. Through the decades when this family could not safely keep its name, it went on keeping the festival that named it anyway. The surname went into hiding. The worship did not.

Gaps

What we do not know

We do not know what the woman in the first row was called. We do not know what Rameshwar Nath did with his hands, when he was born, or when he stopped. The ancestral house in Gaya is still standing, and nobody left in my line can walk to its door.

That is what a family is, after a while: a chain of people who were certain of things that nobody thought to write down. This page exists to stop a little more of it going the same way.
